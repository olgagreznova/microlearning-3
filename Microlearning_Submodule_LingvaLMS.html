3 Reflective Framework & Discussion

🧠 How to justify your teaching decisions

When designing a microlearning activity, a foreign language teacher should be able to explain why a particular teaching method, digital tool or learning task has been selected. Use the following three stages to analyse and justify your decisions.

1. WHAT? – Identify your teaching decision

Describe: First, identify what you are planning to do and what you want your learners to achieve.

Ask yourself:

  • What language skill or learning objective am I targeting?
  • What do I want my learners to know or be able to do?
  • What microlearning activity, digital tool or resource have I selected?
  • What exactly will learners do during the activity?

Useful phrases:

  • I decided to focus on …
  • My main learning objective is to …
  • I chose to use … as I want my learners to …

2. SO WHAT? – Explain and justify your decision

Analyse: Now explain why you believe your choice is pedagogically appropriate. Connect your decision with research, teaching principles and your learners' needs.

Ask yourself:

  • Why did I choose this particular activity or tool?
  • What pedagogical principles support my decision?
  • What does research say about this approach?
  • Why is this format appropriate for my learners' language level and needs?

Useful phrases:

  • I made this choice because …
  • From a pedagogical perspective, …
  • According to [author] ([year]), …
  • Research findings suggest that …

3. NOW WHAT? – Evaluate and improve your decision

Reflect and act: Finally, consider whether your decision is realistic and how you could improve it. Remember that even a well-designed activity may not work equally well for every learner.

Ask yourself:

  • What difficulties might learners encounter?
  • What are the possible limitations of my chosen activity?
  • How will I know whether the learning objective has been achieved?
  • What could I change if the activity does not work as expected?

Useful phrases:

  • One possible limitation of my approach is …
  • To address this limitation, I would …
  • I could improve the activity by …
  • In future lessons, I would consider …

Example Structure:
"I decided to design a five-minute vocabulary activity using digital flashcards. My main objective is to help B1 learners remember new collocations and use them in spoken communication (WHAT). I chose digital flashcards because they allow learners to practise recalling vocabulary in short sessions. This is relevant to my learners because they can recognise collocations but struggle to retrieve them when speaking (SO WHAT). One possible limitation of flashcards is that learners may recognise the correct answer without being able to use the collocations independently. To address this, I would include a follow-up speaking task in which learners have to use the new expressions in their own sentences (NOW WHAT)."

📝 Final discussion task: Justify Your Teaching Choices

Your role: You are a foreign language teacher designing a microlearning activity for B1-level English learners aged 16–18.
Your students: recognise new vocabulary but often struggle to remember it and use it spontaneously in speaking.

Discussion question:

How would you design a microlearning activity to help your students remember and actively use new vocabulary?

Your instructions:

  1. Write a response of approximately 150–200 words.
  2. Structure your argument around the three stages: WHAT?, SO WHAT? and NOW WHAT?
  3. Support at least one teaching decision with a research finding or pedagogical principle.
  4. Consider your learners' needs and identify at least one possible limitation of your approach.
  5. Read your classmates' responses and choose one peer's contribution.
  6. Write a reply of approximately 50–80 words. Explain whether you agree with their teaching decision, ask a relevant question or suggest an improvement. Give a pedagogical reason for your comment.
Your final goal: Demonstrate that you can make informed teaching decisions, justify them using pedagogical knowledge and research, and critically reflect on your own teaching practice.
Theoretical reference: Sellars, M. (2017). Reflective Practice for Teachers (2nd ed.). SAGE Publications.
